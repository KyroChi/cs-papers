Who Fills arXiv

An interactive look at the growth of arXiv and the machine-learning conferences.

Data — arXiv metadata (3.15M papers): secemp9/arxiv-complete (Hugging Face; metadata config, Cornell/CC0). Conference affiliations (NeurIPS·ICML·ICLR): Paper Copilot OpenReview archive (arXiv:2510.13201).

Key findings

  • arXiv submissions have grown ~9× since 2000 (≈31k → ≈284k in 2025) and are still accelerating.Fig 1a
  • The rise is driven by Computer Science — specifically the AI/ML subfields — and by teams, not solo authors.Fig 1bFig 2Fig 3
  • At the top ML venues, China's submission share rose steeply (~13% → ~23% by 2025; level with the US only in the partial-2026 ICLR-only cycle), driven mainly by a ~9× rise in PhD-student first authors — not students each writing more.Fig 6Fig 7

All of arXiv

  • Submissions grew ~9× from 2000 (≈31k) to 2025 (≈284k), fastest in the most recent years (≈+16%/yr in 2024–25).Fig 1a
  • Computer Science overtook physics to become the largest area; its share of arXiv climbed steeply as physics and math shares shrank.Fig 1b
  • Growth is collaborative: solo-authored papers fell from ~44% (1991) to ~11% (2025); the median author count rose from 2 to 4.Fig 2

Computer Science

  • CS is the single biggest driver of arXiv's rise — from 27 papers (1995) to ~129k (2025), now the largest area.Fig 1b
  • CS is even more team-heavy than arXiv overall; solo work is a small and shrinking sliver.Fig 2b
  • Growth concentrates in the AI/ML subfields — the top-5 in 2025 are cs.CV, cs.LG, cs.CL, cs.AI, cs.RO.Fig 3
  • Mean papers per CS author per year is rising (≈2.0→2.5, 2021–25) with a growing right tail (s.d. up), while the median stays 1.Fig 4
  • Prolific authors are getting more prolific: those with 21+ career papers went from ~8 to ~11 papers/yr; occasional authors are flat.Fig 5

By region — NeurIPS · ICML · ICLR

  • China's share rose sharply: China-majority submissions went ~13% → ~23% (2021→2025) as the US fell ~49% → ~37%, "Other" near 40% — the gap narrowed (they cross only in the partial-2026 ICLR-only data; see the 2026 section).Fig 6aFig 6b
  • Indexed to 2021, China's submissions grew ~10× vs ~4× for the US (through 2025).Fig 6c
  • At the first-author level (who led the paper), PhD students dominate in every region; switching to "all authors" pulls faculty/PIs up — the two bases tell different stories.Fig 7
  • China's PhD-led surge is headcount, not output: ~9× more distinct PhD first-authors, with papers-per-student flat (~1.2) and a stable seniority ratio. The US shows a mild per-student rise (1.2→1.45).Fig 7
  • The inflow of new PhD-student first-authors grows fastest for China (~5× over 2022–25 vs ~2.6× for the US), though the US still added more per year through 2025.Fig 7c
  • First-author PhD submissions grew ~9.9× for China (285→2,821) vs ~4.1× for the US (1,413→5,853) over 2021–25 — China's first-author PhD output is growing ~2.4× faster, though the US base is larger.Fig 7e
  • Faculty are on the most papers per year (mean ≈3 in 2025) vs PhD students ≈1.9, but the median is 1 for every tier — the gap is a prolific minority, not typical behavior.Fig 7d

Mathematics

  • Even math is going collaborative: solo papers fell 52% → 29% (2000→2025) while 3+-author papers rose 15% → 38% — the growth is multi-author, not solo.Fig 8
  • Top-5 2026 subfields: combinatorics (math.CO), analysis of PDEs (math.AP), optimization & control (math.OC), numerical analysis (math.NA), number theory (math.NT).Fig 9
  • Per-author output is roughly flat (mean ≈1.6→1.7, median 1) — far below CS.Fig 10
  • Unlike CS, math's most prolific authors are not speeding up — the 21+ bucket's output drifted down (~4.6 → 3.1 papers/yr).Fig 11

Peer review — ICLR (submissions incl. rejected)

  • Acceptance kept pace with submissions. ICLR submissions grew ~24× (489 in 2017 → 11,663 in 2025), but the acceptance rate held ~30–32% since 2019 — so accepted papers scaled up too (~200 → ~3,700, ~19×), rather than staying fixed.Fig 12Fig 13
  • The total-submissions bar explodes while the accepted slice stays a roughly constant share — the conference raised its accepted count, not its selectivity.Fig 12
  • ICLR 2026 decisions aren't released yet, so acceptance is shown through 2025.

Preliminary notes — is it more students, or students writing more?

  • Mostly more students. PhD-student submissions (all authorships) grew ~6.1× (2021→2025), decomposing into ~4.5× more distinct students × ~1.34× more papers per student — headcount is the dominant term.Fig 14a
  • Per-student output rose ~34% for all authorship (1.40 → 1.88) but only ~16% for first-authorship (1.18 → 1.37) — so the extra is mostly co-authorship, not students each leading more papers. First-author PhD growth is ~4.2× headcount. (A per-student productivity effect — e.g. AI-assisted writing — would surface here; the modest first-author rise is where to watch, but it's too early and confounded to attribute.)Fig 14b
  • The median is 1 paper/year for students throughout — the typical PhD student still submits one paper a year; the mean rises from a growing tail.Fig 7d
  • Caveats (why this is preliminary): OpenReview coverage itself grew over 2021–25, inflating headcount; ML-venues only; positions are self-reported; the window is short and starts at 2021. Directional, not causal.

Controlling for LLMs — per-person output, deep history

  • Counting first-authorships only (which controls for growing team size), output per person was flat-to-declining through 2022 — CS held ~1.35 for 18 years. There was no secular productivity trend before LLMs.Fig 16
  • A modest uptick begins in 2023 — strongest in CS (~1.35 → ~1.45, +7%), weak across all fields, negligible in math. It's concentrated in the heaviest-LLM-use field and timed to ChatGPT (late 2022), not GPT-3 (2020).Fig 16
  • Suggestive, not conclusive. The bump is small and confounded (paper-splitting, LLMs-as-research-topic inflating CS, workshop tracks). First-author-per-person removes team-size effects but not these. Deep arXiv history (from 1991) gives the pre-LLM control that the 2021+ conference data cannot.

Is the surge just an LLM artifact?

  • The steelman, taken at face value. For the sake of argument, assume the entire post-2022 per-person uptick (Fig 16) is 100% caused by LLMs — every extra fraction of a paper per author is AI-enabled.
  • Even then, it's a minor term. CS papers grew ~2× (64.7k → 128.9k, 2022→2025). Of that, the number of first authors rose ×1.87 while output per person rose only ×1.067. Strip the per-person rise entirely and CS still grows to ~121k — so LLM productivity could explain at most ~10–13% of the increase; ~87–90% is simply more people.Fig 16Fig 14a
  • Timing rules out the earlier growth. Per-person output was flat through 2022, yet arXiv had already grown ~9× since 2000. LLMs cannot account for a surge that predates them by two decades.Fig 1aFig 16
  • Not just an LLM spam flood. If the extra papers were mostly low-quality AI filler, acceptance rates should have collapsed. Instead ICLR's rate held ~30–32% and accepted counts scaled with submissions — the added volume clears review at the same bar.Fig 13
  • Bottom line. The increase is primarily a people phenomenon — more researchers, especially PhD students in ML, on larger teams. LLMs are at most a small, recent multiplier on top, not the primary cause; the headcount, China-growth, and team-size findings hold regardless.

2026 so far (through August, year-to-date)

  • Through August 2026, arXiv already logged 225,912 submissions — ~24% ahead of the same Jan–Aug window in 2025 (181,969). On pace for another record year.Fig 15
  • Computer Science is now ~48% of Jan–Aug submissions (2026), up from 46% (2025) and 34% (2021) — nearly half of all new arXiv papers.Fig 15
  • Conferences (preliminary, ICLR-only — NeurIPS/ICML 2026 not yet released): ICLR 2026 drew ~22.8k submissions (vs 13.7k in 2025); by author-majority region China (~31%) edged past the US (~30%) for the first time — one venue, decisions still pending.
  • 2026 is partial and excluded from the figures above; treat it as a leading indicator.

Methods & data

Data sources
arXiv metadata for 3.15M papers from the metadata config of secemp9/arxiv-complete (Hugging Face; a 1.6 GB parquet, not the full 16 TiB corpus). arXiv metadata is distributed by Cornell under CC0. Conference author affiliations (NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR) from the Paper Copilot OpenReview archive (arXiv:2510.13201). ICLR accept/reject decisions from the berenslab/iclr-dataset. All data is fetched by scripts; none is redistributed here.
Author counting
The raw author string is cleaned before splitting: we drop the trailing numbered-affiliation block (e.g. “((1) INAF, Italy, (2) …)”), strip remaining inline “(affiliation)” parentheticals, then split on commas and the word “and” and discard “et al”. Without this, affiliation and country fragments leak in as false authors. A named collaboration (e.g. “ATLAS Collaboration”) counts as one author. This is a proxy, imperfect for unusual formatting.
Author identity & de-duplication
On arXiv, authors are identified by name string, so common names collide (“Y. Wang” merges many people) — per-author figures are directional. Conference figures de-duplicate by OpenReview profile id (or_profile), which is reliable.
Subject groups
arXiv archives are collapsed into coarse groups (Computer Science, Physics, Mathematics, Statistics, EESS, …) by primary category; the smallest groups fold into “Other”. Sub-discipline figures use the raw primary_category.
Institution region
From the author's OpenReview institutional email domain (the local part is masked). .cn and major Chinese firms → China; .edu/US firms → US; other ccTLDs → their country; the rest → Other. Company domains map to HQ country, not office location (a Google-Beijing author counts as US), so an academia-only cut is cleaner. Personal-email domains (gmail, qq, …) are treated as unknown-institution, not a company. ~11% of domains are unclassifiable and excluded from region shares.
Paper-level region attribution
Each paper is assigned to the region holding the most of its authors' institutions; ties are broken by a deterministic hash of the paper id (a “fair coin” that is uniform in aggregate but reproducible). ~6–7% of papers are ties.
Seniority
Self-reported OpenReview position (“PhD student”, “Full Professor”, …) mapped to tiers — not inferred from paper counts. Figures distinguish a first-author basis (each paper counted once, by who led it) from an all-authors basis (every authorship; a PI on many papers is tallied each time).
Coverage & time windows
arXiv spans 1991–2025 (main figures end at 2025, the last complete year; 2026 is year-to-date through August, shown separately). Conference affiliation/position data populates from ~2021. ICLR includes all submissions incl. rejected; NeurIPS/ICML are largely accepted + opt-in, so pooled conference numbers mix submissions and acceptances. ICLR 2026 decisions are not yet released.
Bucket definitions
Author-count and career-total buckets are made non-overlapping where the requested edges overlapped (e.g. author counts {1, 2–3, 4–5, 6–8, 9+}; CS career totals {1, 2–3, 4–8, 9–20, 21+}; Math author counts {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6+}).
LLM-control metric
To separate productivity from team growth, per-person output is measured as first-author papers per person per year (each paper counted once). arXiv's deep history provides a pre-LLM baseline; the conference data (2021+) does not.
Reproducibility
Open-source: download_*.py fetch the data, build_figures.py computes the aggregates via DuckDB, and build_html.py assembles this self-contained page. Raw data is git-ignored.
Caveats
All findings are directional, not causal: name/position self-reporting, domain-to-country heuristics, ML-venue-only conference coverage, OpenReview coverage that itself grows over time, and short post-2021 windows all introduce noise. Treat magnitudes as approximate and trends as the signal.
